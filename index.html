Institutionalised Plunder

Law, Power, and the Systematic Extraction of Wealth

Institutionalised plunder—also rendered as institutionalized plunder—describes the transformation of extraction from episodic violence or opportunistic theft into a durable, rule-governed process. It occurs when laws, administrative structures, property regimes, financial systems, and international institutions convert the taking of resources, labour, or value from weaker parties into something that appears legitimate, orderly, and even necessary.

Ordinary theft is easy to recognise because it happens against the rules. Institutionalised plunder is harder to identify because it may operate through the rules themselves. The transfer is mediated by legislation, taxation, contracts, courts, licences, concessions, debt, property titles, or regulatory systems. Each individual act may look routine. What matters is the structure they create together.

The central question is therefore not simply whether a transfer is legal. It is who wrote the rule, whose claims the rule recognises, who carries the cost, who receives the benefit, and whether those affected have any meaningful power to challenge the arrangement. Formal legality can restrain power, but it can also stabilise it.

Bastiat and the idea of legal plunder

Frédéric Bastiat gave one of the earliest and clearest formulations of this problem. In The Law (1850), he argued that law becomes corrupted when it performs the very act it is supposed to prevent: taking property from one person and transferring it to another. He called this “legal plunder.”1

Bastiat’s own political conclusions were strongly classical-liberal. He treated tariffs, subsidies, redistribution, and several forms of state intervention as examples of legal plunder. That broader position remains controversial and does not have to be accepted in full. The useful part of his argument is more basic: once political authority can create economic advantages, control over political authority itself becomes economically valuable.

This is close to what later theories would describe as rent-seeking or regulatory capture. A group does not necessarily need to steal from the public directly. It can seek a protected market, a favourable tax rule, a monopoly licence, a procurement advantage, a regulatory exemption, or a legal structure that transfers risk to others. The extraction becomes more durable because it is attached to an institution rather than to a single act.

Bastiat’s framework, however, becomes too broad if every compulsory transfer is treated as theft. Modern states tax in order to finance courts, infrastructure, public health, education, security, social insurance, and other collective goods. A useful concept of institutionalised plunder therefore needs more than coercion. It requires attention to power asymmetry, concentrated benefit, weak accountability, limited contestation, and the repeated transfer of value toward actors capable of shaping the rules.

Colonialism: extraction made administrative

Colonialism provides one of the clearest historical examples. Conquest could seize territory, but conquest alone could not create a stable system of extraction. Durable control required administration. Land had to be surveyed. Ownership had to be defined. Revenue had to be assessed. Courts had to determine which claims were valid. Trade had to be regulated. Concessions had to be granted. Labour had to be organised, taxed, or controlled.

Ugo Mattei and Laura Nader developed this argument in Plunder: When the Rule of Law is Illegal. Their central point is that the “rule of law” cannot be evaluated only by asking whether officials follow formal procedures. Law itself can become part of an extractive project when stronger actors construct the legal categories and institutions through which weaker actors lose resources, control, or bargaining power.2

British rule in Bengal shows how this could work. After the East India Company obtained revenue-collecting rights in Bengal in 1765, the colonial state gradually reorganised land revenue, property claims, courts, and administration. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 was not merely a tax decision. It helped formalise a new structure of landholding and revenue collection in which older and more fluid relationships were translated into categories that colonial institutions could recognise and enforce.3

The important point is not that every colonial legal reform had one simple motive. Institutions often produce several effects at the same time. The point is that extraction becomes far more durable when it is converted into recognised rights and administrative routines. A military seizure can be resisted as an act of force. A title deed, revenue claim, concession, or court judgment can make the same distribution appear normal and permanent.

This also explains why the effects of colonial systems may survive political independence. States inherit boundaries, land records, bureaucracies, fiscal systems, commercial law, resource concessions, and administrative habits. Some are reformed, some are retained, and some are repurposed by domestic elites. Institutional continuity does not require a continuing colonial conspiracy. Path dependence is enough: rules created under one balance of power can continue to shape another.

State capture: changing the rules rather than breaking them

In contemporary political economy, the closest established concept is state capture. A World Bank study by Joel Hellman, Geraint Jones, and Daniel Kaufmann distinguished ordinary administrative corruption from situations in which firms or powerful interests shape the formation of laws, regulations, and policies to obtain private advantage.4

The difference is important. A bribe may be paid to evade a rule. State capture aims higher: it alters the rule itself. The tender specification, tax exemption, regulatory standard, appointment, licensing regime, or enforcement priority can be designed so that the transfer appears procedurally normal when it finally occurs.

This is one reason institutionalised plunder can be difficult to expose. A corrupt act may leave evidence of illegality. A captured institution may produce documents that are perfectly legal on their face. The real question lies upstream: how was the rule produced, who had access to the process, and who was excluded?

The 1Malaysia Development Berhad (1MDB) scandal shows a related but somewhat different mechanism. According to the U.S. Department of Justice, more than US$4.5 billion was allegedly misappropriated from 1MDB between 2009 and 2015 through an international scheme involving money laundering and embezzlement. By June 2024, the Department reported that approximately US$1.4 billion had been returned or assisted in being returned to Malaysia.5

1MDB involved alleged criminal conduct, so it should not be treated as a pure example of lawful extraction. Its relevance is institutional. Large-scale diversion of public wealth depended not only on individuals but on corporate vehicles, financial institutions, professional intermediaries, cross-border transactions, and legal ownership structures. Modern extraction often operates through networks in which public and private institutions reinforce one another.

When public institutions depend on extraction

Another form appears when public institutions develop a financial dependence on the people they regulate or punish. The U.S. Department of Justice’s investigation of Ferguson, Missouri, documented a municipal system in which policing and court practices were heavily oriented toward raising revenue through fines and fees. The city collected more than US$1.3 million in court fines and fees in 2010 and budgeted more than US$3 million in fine revenue for fiscal year 2015. The Department concluded that pressure to increase revenue contributed to unconstitutional practices.6

The importance of this example lies in its ordinariness. The extraction did not depend on a single dramatic seizure. It occurred through tickets, warrants, fees, court procedures, payment obligations, and repeated enforcement. Small individual burdens accumulated into a system that generated revenue from a population with limited ability to absorb or contest those burdens.

This gives us an important diagnostic principle: an institution becomes especially vulnerable to extractive behaviour when its survival, budget, or incentives depend on the penalties, fees, forfeitures, rents, or concessions it imposes. Ethics rules may punish individual abuse, but they do not remove the underlying incentive.

The international legal order

Similar questions arise at the international level, particularly around debt, investment treaties, intellectual property, and investor–State dispute settlement (ISDS). Here the argument needs more caution. International investment law can provide real benefits. Investors may require protection against arbitrary expropriation, discriminatory treatment, or abrupt changes in the legal environment. Predictable dispute resolution can reduce risk and encourage investment.

But legal protection is also a form of power. UN Trade and Development reported that by the end of 2024 there were 1,401 known treaty-based investor–State arbitration cases. Around 55% of the new cases filed in 2024 were brought against developing countries, and more than half of the new cases related to extractive activities and energy supply.7

These figures do not prove that ISDS is itself a system of plunder. They show why the architecture deserves scrutiny. If one class of actors has access to strong international remedies while affected communities, workers, taxpayers, or environmental interests have weaker enforcement mechanisms, the legal order may distribute bargaining power unevenly. Whether that becomes exploitative depends on the treaty, the dispute, the obligations involved, and the ability of states to preserve legitimate regulatory space.

How institutionalised plunder actually works

Across these different examples, several mechanisms recur.

First is rule-making power. Actors who can influence legislation, regulation, concessions, tax rules, procurement, or treaties can transform temporary political advantage into a durable economic entitlement.

Second is recognition power. Legal systems decide which claims count. A registered title may defeat customary tenure. A formal creditor may have stronger remedies than an informal claimant. A licensed operator may have rights that a local user does not. Law does not merely enforce claims; it decides which claims become visible.

Third is enforcement asymmetry. Two parties may possess formally equal rights while having completely different capacities to use them. One may have lawyers, experts, money, institutional access, and the ability to delay. The other may have a right on paper but no realistic way to enforce it.

Fourth is fiscal dependence. When an institution benefits financially from the penalties, seizures, fees, or concessions it administers, extraction can become part of its organisational logic.

Fifth is complexity. Modern extraction rarely arrives labelled as extraction. It is hidden inside technical contracts, corporate structures, debt instruments, procurement rules, regulatory language, or international agreements. Complexity raises the cost of understanding the transfer and therefore reduces effective resistance.

Finally, there is legitimation. Extractive systems survive more easily when they are presented as development, modernisation, efficiency, security, fiscal necessity, market discipline, or legal order. These justifications may sometimes be genuine. The analytical task is not to dismiss them automatically, but to compare the claimed public purpose with the actual distribution of benefits and costs.

Not every unequal system is plunder

The concept becomes meaningless if it is used for every tax, regulation, unpopular policy, foreign investment, or unequal transaction. Inequality alone is not enough. Coercion alone is not enough. Even concentrated benefits are not enough by themselves.

A better test asks several questions together. Was the transfer produced by a major power asymmetry? Are the benefits concentrated while the costs fall on weaker or less organised groups? Could those groups meaningfully contest the arrangement? Was the rule designed or captured to create private advantage? Does the transfer repeat itself because the institution depends on it? Is there a credible public-purpose justification, and is that justification supported by the actual outcomes?

These distinctions matter. A transparent tax imposed through an accountable political system and used for broad public services is institutionally different from a levy designed by captured officials to benefit a narrow group. At the same time, a formally voluntary contract is not automatically fair if monopoly, dependency, or inherited dispossession leaves one side without meaningful alternatives.

Why it persists

Institutionalised plunder is durable because institutions reduce the need for visible coercion. Once the transfer has been converted into a rule, future transfers can happen automatically. The law is enforced by ordinary officials. The contract renews itself. The fee is collected. The debt is serviced. The licence remains exclusive. The property record continues to be recognised.

This also creates a constituency for continuation. Those who benefit from the arrangement have a reason to defend it, while those who pay the cost may be numerous, dispersed, poorly organised, or unaware of how the system works. A small group can therefore have a much stronger incentive to preserve an advantage than the wider public has to remove it.

Reform usually requires more than changing individuals. Removing a corrupt official does not necessarily change the incentive structure that produced the behaviour. If an agency depends on fines, if a regulator is structurally dependent on the industry it regulates, or if a political system is financed by concentrated economic interests, the same pattern can reappear with different people.

Conclusion

Institutionalised plunder is not simply another word for corruption, inequality, or government intervention. It describes a more specific process: the conversion of power into durable rules that repeatedly transfer value while making the transfer appear normal, legal, or inevitable.

Bastiat’s idea of legal plunder identified the basic problem. Colonial systems showed how law and administration could turn conquest into a stable economic order. Modern theories of state capture explain how powerful interests can shape laws rather than merely violate them. Revenue-driven justice systems show how extractive incentives can become embedded inside public institutions. International legal regimes demonstrate that even neutral-looking rules can distribute legal power unevenly.

None of this means that law is merely an instrument of exploitation. Law remains one of the strongest protections against arbitrary power. But legality is not self-validating. A legal system must also be judged by how rules are made, how benefits and burdens are distributed, and whether those affected have a realistic ability to challenge the institutions governing them.

The deepest danger appears when concentrated power can convert its advantage into a permanent rule and then present that rule as neutral order. Preventing institutionalised plunder therefore requires more than punishing theft after it occurs. It requires examining the structure that makes extraction repeatable, defensible, and difficult to see.
